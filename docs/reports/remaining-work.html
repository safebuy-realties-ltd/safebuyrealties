Remaining work: the six things only the stakeholder can do

Nineteen stories are left on the MVP. Five of them a developer can start this morning. The other fourteen are waiting on six items that no amount of engineering can produce, because they are decisions, purchases, accounts and documents that belong to somebody outside this team.

This is the third edition. The first was written on 1 August. The second was written on the morning of 2 August, when one stakeholder answer was still holding back a third of the build. That answer came back the same day, which is why this edition exists: it released seventeen developer-days, it turned a report about a stalled project into a report about a schedule, and it is the clearest evidence in this document of what one sentence from a stakeholder is worth here.

Generated 2026-08-02, after PRs 134, 135 and 136 merged. Every figure below is derived from docs/mvp-board.html and docs/MVP_OUTSTANDING_BACKLOG.md at main @ 5be4b6b, and the derivation is described under Method at the foot of the page.

The position in one paragraph. Forty of the fifty-nine rows on the board are closed. Of the nineteen that remain, five are free: a developer can pick up the first of them this morning and nobody has to be asked for anything. The other fourteen carry forty-four developer-days and every one of them is stopped in front of the same six items. Those items are not engineering work and they cannot be worked around. They are one decision about money, one decision about where documents live, a mail domain, two legal documents and a booked security test.

What changed since the second edition, and it changed the shape of the report. The stakeholder confirmed that buying a property on the platform is part of the MVP. That is ADR-0001, and answering it released five stories and seventeen developer-days in a single sentence, at a cost to the person answering of one reply. Before it, this page reported a runway of zero: there was literally nothing a developer could begin. Now there are four calendar days of building in front of the team that need nobody's permission.

What has not changed, and it is now the whole story. Every one of the remaining six items has a lead time attached to it, and five of the six will arrive after the day the code in front of them is ready. Three of them take between two and three weeks to obtain, and between them they hold nine developer-days, which is about one day of building. So the finish date of this MVP is not being set by how much code is left. It is being set by when six emails get sent.

Before the figures: what every code on this page means

This project labels things tersely so that the board, the backlog and this report can all point at the same thing without ambiguity. That helps the people who read them daily and it slows down everybody else, so the whole vocabulary is written out here. Nothing below needs to be looked up anywhere else, and every code used in a figure is also spelled out where the figure uses it.

What each prefix means

ADR
Architecture Decision Record. A numbered, written-down decision, kept in docs/adr/. It exists so that a choice is made once, by a named person, with the reasoning attached, instead of being made five times by whoever is typing. An ADR that is still open is a piece of engineering that nobody is allowed to start.
EXT
External input. Something the team cannot produce with code no matter how many people are on it: an account with a supplier, a domain, a signed document, a booked appointment. Numbered EXT-1 to EXT-7.
E1 to E8
Epic. A theme of work. The eight of them are listed on the right.
-S1, -S2
Story within that epic. E6-S2 is the second story of epic 6. A story is the unit that becomes one pull request.
S, M, L
Size. Small is about a day, Medium two to four days, Large about a week. These are developer-days of scope, not calendar days, and the difference matters enough that figure 6 is about nothing else.
D1 to D5
Decision awaiting an answer. Each one maps to an ADR.
G1 to G6
Go-live gate. A condition that must be demonstrably true before launch. Listed on the right.
W1 to W4
Wave. A batch of stories a developer works through together. Waves 1, 2 and 3 are closed. Wave 4 is open.
QA-nnn
A numbered finding from the quality audit that produced this backlog.
DOCS-n, CH-n
Documentation and chore rows. Real pull requests that carry no engineering days, so they never move the totals in this report.

The eight epics, in plain words

E1 Loop
Close the loop. Make buying a property on the platform actually work end to end. This is the wave that just opened.
E2 Money
Money integrity. Sellers get paid into their own account, refunds genuinely reach the buyer's card, and finance can reconcile it.
E3 Trust
Document trust. Uploaded documents survive a deploy, and a file claiming to be a PDF is checked rather than believed.
E4 Access
Access correctness. The right people see the right things, and identity checks actually gate the actions that move money.
E5 Security
Account security. Password reset, email verification, lockout, session handling.
E6 Comms
Communications. The platform can send an email. Today it cannot.
E7 Ops
Operability. The things that make the system runnable: health checks, logging, a demo that can be reset.
E8 Compliance
Compliance and go-live. Privacy law, counsel-approved legal text, an independent security review.

The six go-live gates

G1
A buyer completes the on-platform journey on staging with no staff intervention. Now measurable, because E1 is finally allowed to be built.
G2
A test payout reaches a real, distinct seller account and a test refund is repaid by the payment gateway. Waits on E2.
G3

G4

G5

G6

Words borrowed from finance, law and email

escrow
Holding a buyer's money in a third account until both sides have done their part, instead of passing it straight to the seller. Whether this platform does that is ADR-0002, and it is a regulatory question as much as a technical one.
PoA
Power of Attorney. The instrument by which a buyer authorises the platform to act on their behalf. Its wording was drafted by engineers in a build checklist, which is why EXT-4 asks counsel to look at it.
NDPR
Nigeria Data Protection Regulation. The law governing what this platform may do with personal data. It is why E8-S1 exists, and E8-S1 stores identity documents and selfies today with no consent model, no retention policy and no way to erase anybody.
KYC
Know Your Customer. The identity check a regulated business performs before letting somebody move money. The records exist here; nothing reads them yet.
AML / CBN
Anti-Money Laundering obligations, and the Central Bank of Nigeria, the regulator whose rules decide what an escrow answer is allowed to be.
SPF, DKIM
Two DNS records that tell the rest of the internet that mail claiming to come from this domain really does. Without them, the platform's mail lands in spam or is rejected outright. They are part of EXT-3 and they take minutes to add.
pen test
Penetration test. A paid, independent attempt to break into the system, run by people who did not build it. EXT-6 is booking one.
reconciliation
Checking that what the platform thinks it moved and what the bank actually moved are the same number. E2-S5 is the screen that does it.
retention and erasure
How long personal data is kept, and how a person gets it deleted. Both are legal positions before they are code, which is why EXT-5 comes first.

Every decision and external input, by name

The six on this page are marked in red. The rest are here so that a code seen elsewhere in the repository can be looked up in one place.

ADR-0001
Answered on 2 August. Is buying a property on the platform part of the MVP? Yes. That answer is the reason this edition exists. It released E1's four stories and, behind them, E4-S2. Recorded as Accepted in docs/adr/0001-mvp-scope-envelope.md.
ADR-0002
Does the platform hold client funds, or pass them straight through? The half of EXT-7 still outstanding. It decides whether E2-S1 is a bank details form or a regulated money flow, so it cannot be guessed at and then corrected later. Holds three stories and gate G6.
ADR-0003
Payment mock-mode guard. Decided and shipped. Production can no longer start in a mode that pretends payments succeeded.
ADR-0004
How are private documents served? Signed short-lived links from object storage, or streamed through the API with an authorization check on every read. It has to be decided before the bucket is bought, because the two answers need different bucket settings. Holds three stories and half of gate G3.
ADR-0005
The quality ratchet. Decided and enforced. Coverage on changed lines cannot fall.
EXT-1
Production payment keys and a settlement account. Travels with ADR-0002: the decision has to be made before the account can be opened, and the account has a business verification behind it.
EXT-2
An S3-compatible bucket, its credentials and a region choice. Travels with ADR-0004.
EXT-3
A transactional email domain, SPF, DKIM, DMARC and sending credentials. The single highest-value item on this page: it holds thirteen developer-days and it is the fastest of the six to obtain.
EXT-4
Counsel-approved Power of Attorney text and terms of service. Holds one story worth a single day, and gate G4.
EXT-5
An NDPR privacy notice and a retention policy. Holds the largest single blocked story on the board.
EXT-6
A penetration test vendor and a booked window. Holds one story worth a single day, and gate G5.
EXT-7
Confirmation of D1 and D2. Half discharged on 2 August, when D1 came back. What is left of it is D2, which is ADR-0002.

A developer-day is a unit of scope, not a unit of elapsed time, and on this project the two are far apart. One developer running many agents in parallel, with a second developer reviewing and merging, does not spend those days one after another. The evidence is this repository's own history.

Where the rate comes from. Eleven pull requests merged into main on 31 July and eleven more on 1 August. Wave 1 was four stories and eight published developer-days, and it merged as PRs 119 to 122 inside a single afternoon. Wave 3 was four stories and eighteen developer-days in a day. The ceiling this report plans against is six merges and nineteen developer-days in a calendar day, which is the highest the repository has actually sustained, and every day in figure 7 sits under it.

What that rate cannot buy. It cannot compress a chain. Wave 4 is four stories deep and each one waits on the one before it, so it takes four calendar days no matter how many agents are pointed at it. Adding people or agents to a chain of four does not make it a chain of one. This is why the runway figure at the top of the page is four days rather than one.

Method

Where the numbers come from. The story list, its statuses, its dependencies and the per-epic effort ranges are read directly from docs/mvp-board.html and docs/MVP_OUTSTANDING_BACKLOG.md. The board is checked on every pull request by scripts/check-board.mjs, which fails if the totals on it stop agreeing with the rows underneath them, so the inputs to this page are gated rather than trusted.

How per-story days are derived. The backlog publishes effort as a range per epic, not per story. Each epic's upper bound is apportioned across its remaining stories in proportion to size, with Small counting one, Medium three and Large five, and any remainder given to the largest story in the epic. Every allocation lands inside its published size band. The result sums to sixty-one, which is exactly the board's published ceiling.

A two-day difference from the last edition, and it is not new work. The second edition's per-story figures summed to fifty-nine against a published ceiling of sixty-one, and it called the gap drift and left it. The gap has now been located: it was two developer-days lost to rounding, one in E7 and one in E8, when the remainder was spread evenly instead of given to the largest story. Fixing the arithmetic added two days to the total on paper and added no work at all.

Upper bounds throughout. Every figure on this page uses the ceiling of each range, not the floor. The floor assumes every remaining story lands at the cheap end of its estimate, and no week in this repository has done that.

Lead times. The calendar figures for obtaining each external item are planning estimates, not quotes. They are deliberately optimistic: one day for a mail domain, two for a storage decision and a bucket, twelve for a payment decision with a business verification behind it, fourteen for counsel, twenty-one for a privacy review and for a penetration test window. If any of them is wrong it is wrong in the direction of being too short, which makes the case in figure 4 stronger rather than weaker.

How the work is staffed. One developer building with as many AI agents in parallel as the work allows, and a second developer reviewing and merging. That arrangement is why the calendar figures in figure 6 sit so far below the developer-day figures, and it is also why the stakeholder items dominate this report: when building gets fast, waiting is all that is left.
